\subsection{Link quality}

Two numbers describe the interlinking. \emph{Precision} is measured by hand on
the seeded 50-link sample and is \textbf{[XX\%]} at the time of writing, against
a project target of 90\%; Section~\ref{sec:interlinking} explains the procedure and reports the
false positive found in the sample. \emph{Recall} is deliberately uneven
(Table~\ref{tab:links}): 100\% for distilleries and brands, whose identifiers
come from the source; 84.6\% for ingredients; and 20.4\% for cocktails, where
exact-label matching and the high acceptance threshold reject ambiguous names.
Overall, 1{,}220 of 1{,}617 entities (75.4\%) carry a Wikidata link, and 32.0\%
of those also carry a DBpedia link. This is a precision-first trade-off: a
wrong \texttt{owl:sameAs} assertion pollutes the graph with false facts, while a
missing one merely limits coverage.

\subsection{Limitations}

\begin{itemize}[itemsep=1pt]
  \item \textbf{Base-spirit heuristic.} Only 76\% of cocktails received a
        classified base spirit, and the first-match rule can label a drink by
        the wrong ingredient when a recipe lists an unusual component first.
  \item \textbf{Thin brand coverage.} Only 12 Brands were collected, because
        Wikidata rarely records a manufacturer that is itself a distillery.
  \item \textbf{Reconciliation recall and false positives.} Exact-label
        matching cannot handle aliases or qualifiers, and the reward keywords
        can accept a same-named brand or product as in the Martini case.
  \item \textbf{Prose measures.} Quantities that are not numeric at the start
        of the string (``Juice of 1/2'') remain text only.
  \item \textbf{Static dereferencing.} GitHub Pages cannot perform content
        negotiation, so the public URI resolves but downloads its RDF; correct
        negotiation is demonstrated by the local server.
  \item \textbf{Single cocktail source.} All cocktail facts come from
        TheCocktailDB; cross-source validation was out of scope.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Future work}

The clearest improvement is a fuzzy reconciliation layer --- OpenRefine's
reconciliation service, Silk or LIMES --- with manual confirmation, which would
raise cocktail recall without sacrificing precision. A second direction is to
materialise the inferred \texttt{hasIngredient} triples with an OWL reasoner
(for example Jena's rule engine) and compare the inferred graph with the
property-path answers used today. The unused \texttt{FlavorProfile} class could
be populated from taste vocabularies, and measures could be normalised to a
single unit to support aggregation. Finally, loading the three graphs as named
graphs would enable provenance-style queries that separate asserted from
inferred facts, and a larger hand-checked sample would tighten the precision
estimate.

\subsection{Lessons learned}

Three lessons stand out. First, \emph{verify external identifiers}: the
distillery QID taken from the plan denoted a street, and only a check against
the source caught it. Second, \emph{reproducibility is a feature}: cached
downloads, a hash manifest and deterministic URIs made every number in this
report re-derivable, and quality gates caught regressions early. Third,
\emph{portability deserves testing}: the same SPARQL queries required prefix
changes and a trigonometry-free rewrite before they ran on both rdflib and
Jena, a difference that would otherwise have surfaced during the demonstration.
